# Feeding cats

## Feeding cats

Feeding a cat well means three things:

* Choose complete, balanced food.
* Feed the right amount for age and body condition.
* Keep water available at all times.

Most healthy adult cats do best with measured meals, not unlimited food.

### Start with the basics

Cats are obligate carnivores. They need animal-based nutrients, including:

* Protein
* Taurine
* Fatty acids
* Vitamins and minerals from a complete diet

Use food labeled **complete and balanced** for the cat's life stage:

* Kitten
* Adult
* Senior

If a cat is pregnant, nursing, underweight, overweight, or ill, the feeding plan changes.

{% hint style="info" %}
Use the food label and your veterinarian's advice together. Label amounts are a starting point, not a perfect prescription.
{% endhint %}

### Choose the right food

Cats can eat wet food, dry food, or a mix of both.

#### Wet food

Wet food helps increase water intake. Many cats also find it easier to eat.

Use wet food when:

* A cat drinks very little
* You want easier portion control
* A cat prefers softer texture

#### Dry food

Dry food is convenient and stores easily. It is more calorie-dense than wet food.

Use dry food when:

* You need simple measured meals
* The cat prefers kibble
* Cost and storage matter

#### Mixed feeding

Mixed feeding combines wet and dry food. This works well for many homes.

Keep total daily calories consistent. Do not add wet food on top of a full dry ration.

### How much to feed

Daily intake depends on:

* Weight
* Age
* Activity level
* Neuter status
* Body condition
* Food calorie density

For many indoor adult cats, a rough daily energy target is **20 to 25 calories per pound of ideal body weight**. Active cats may need more. Less active or overweight cats may need less.

Examples for healthy adults:

* **8 lb cat:** about 160 to 200 calories per day
* **10 lb cat:** about 200 to 250 calories per day
* **12 lb cat:** about 240 to 300 calories per day

Then convert calories into actual food using the package.

Example:

* Food provides `100 kcal` per 3-ounce can
* Target is `200 kcal` per day
* Feed about `2 cans` per day

Another example:

* Dry food provides `400 kcal` per cup
* Target is `200 kcal` per day
* Feed about `1/2 cup` per day

{% hint style="warning" %}
Do not guess with a scoop or large mug. Measure portions with a proper cup or kitchen scale.
{% endhint %}

### Feed by life stage

#### Kittens

Kittens grow fast and need more energy per pound than adults.

General approach:

* Feed kitten-specific food
* Offer 3 to 4 meals per day
* Increase portions as the kitten grows

Very young kittens that are not weaned need milk replacer, not cow's milk.

#### Adult cats

Most adult cats do well with 2 measured meals per day.

This helps:

* Control calories
* Create routine
* Track appetite changes

#### Senior cats

Senior cats may eat less, lose muscle, or develop health issues.

Watch for:

* Weight loss
* Reduced appetite
* Difficulty chewing
* Vomiting
* Increased thirst

If any appear, adjust the plan with a veterinarian.

### Build a feeding schedule

Use a schedule the cat can predict.

{% stepper %}
{% step %}
### Set meal times

Feed at the same times each day. Morning and evening works well for most adults.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Measure every meal

Split the total daily amount into each meal. Keep everyone in the home consistent.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Remove leftovers when needed

Wet food should not sit out for hours. Refrigerate unused portions and serve fresh.
{% endstep %}

{% step %}
### Track weight and appetite

Weigh the cat regularly. Adjust slowly if weight trends up or down.
{% endstep %}
{% endstepper %}

### Introduce a new food

Change food gradually over 7 to 10 days.

Suggested transition:

* Days 1 to 2: `75%` old food, `25%` new food
* Days 3 to 5: `50%` old food, `50%` new food
* Days 6 to 7: `25%` old food, `75%` new food
* Day 8+: `100%` new food

Slow transitions reduce stomach upset and food refusal.

### Give water every day

Fresh water must always be available.

To encourage drinking:

* Clean bowls daily
* Place bowls away from litter boxes
* Offer multiple water stations
* Consider a cat fountain

Wet food can also help increase total fluid intake.

### Use treats carefully

Treats should stay small. A good rule is **10% or less of daily calories**.

Use treats for:

* Training
* Medication rewards
* Enrichment

Do not let treats replace balanced meals.

### Foods to avoid

Some foods are unsafe or unsuitable for cats.

Avoid:

* Onions, garlic, and chives
* Chocolate
* Grapes and raisins
* Alcohol
* Xylitol
* Caffeine
* Cooked bones
* Raw dough

Limit or avoid:

* Cow's milk
* Fatty table scraps
* Heavily seasoned foods

{% hint style="danger" %}
If a cat eats a toxic food, contact a veterinarian or pet poison service right away.
{% endhint %}

### Feed multiple cats

Multi-cat homes need more structure.

Best practices:

* Feed measured portions
* Use separate bowls
* Supervise meal times
* Use microchip feeders if one cat steals food

This matters even more when cats need different diets.

### Monitor body condition

The food amount is correct only if the cat maintains a healthy body condition.

Signs a cat may be overweight:

* Ribs are hard to feel
* Waist is not visible from above
* Belly hangs noticeably

Signs a cat may be underweight:

* Ribs and spine feel too prominent
* Hip bones stand out
* Muscle loss is visible

Adjust intake in small steps. Recheck every 2 to 4 weeks.

### Special situations

Some cats need a custom plan.

Work with a veterinarian if the cat:

* Has diabetes
* Has kidney disease
* Has urinary issues
* Vomits often
* Has chronic diarrhea
* Stops eating for more than 24 hours

Do not start homemade diets unless they are formulated by a qualified veterinary nutrition professional.

### Common mistakes

Avoid these issues:

* Free-feeding calorie-dense dry food
* Changing food too fast
* Feeding by guesswork
* Giving too many treats
* Ignoring gradual weight gain

Small mistakes add up over time.

### Quick daily checklist

* Feed complete, balanced food
* Measure the full daily amount
* Split it into scheduled meals
* Refresh water
* Watch appetite, stool, and energy

### When to get help

Get veterinary advice if a cat:

* Stops eating
* Loses weight unexpectedly
* Gains weight quickly
* Vomits repeatedly
* Has diarrhea for more than a day
* Drinks or urinates much more than usual

Appetite changes are often an early sign of illness.
