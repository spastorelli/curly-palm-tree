# Legacy and Reconstructions

## Legacy and Reconstructions

Babbage's engines were largely forgotten for decades after his death in 1871. When electronic computing emerged in the 1940s, pioneers rediscovered how completely he had anticipated their ideas.

### Building Difference Engine No. 2

To mark the 200th anniversary of Babbage's birth, the **Science Museum in London** decided to build **Difference Engine No. 2** from his original 1847–49 drawings, using only materials and manufacturing tolerances available in his day.

* The calculating section was completed in **1991** and worked correctly, proving Babbage's design was sound.
* The printing and stereotyping apparatus was finished in **2002**.
* The completed machine consists of around **8,000 parts** and weighs about **5 tonnes**.

A second identical engine was built for the technology entrepreneur Nathan Myhrvold and has been exhibited in the United States.

{% hint style="success" %}
The successful reconstruction demonstrated that, had Babbage received sustained funding and cooperation, a working difference engine was entirely achievable with Victorian technology.
{% endhint %}

### Babbage's lasting influence

* The **Store / Mill** division prefigures the memory / processor split at the heart of every computer.
* **Punched-card control** persisted into the era of early electronic computers.
* **Ada Lovelace's Notes** established computing as a discipline distinct from mathematics. The **Ada** programming language, developed for the U.S. Department of Defense, is named in her honour.

### Where to see the machines

The Science Museum in London displays both a portion of the original Difference Engine No. 1 and the completed Difference Engine No. 2, alongside a section of the Analytical Engine Mill built by Babbage's son. They remain a striking monument to a vision far ahead of its time.
