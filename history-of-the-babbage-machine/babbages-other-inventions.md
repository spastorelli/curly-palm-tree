# Babbage's Other Inventions

## Babbage's Other Inventions

The calculating engines dominate Babbage's reputation, but they were only part of a restless inventive life. He applied the same habit of **careful measurement and mechanical reasoning** to railways, medicine, lighthouses, codes, and even the postal service.

### Railways and the dynamometer car

In 1838–39 Babbage worked with Isambard Kingdom Brunel on the **Great Western Railway**, which was then defending its broad gauge against rival lines. To settle arguments with evidence rather than opinion, Babbage fitted out a railway carriage as a travelling laboratory — a **dynamometer car**.

Pens driven by the carriage's motion traced the forces, vibrations, and jolts of the journey onto long rolls of paper, producing a continuous record of how the train and track behaved. It is often cited as an early forerunner of the automatic data recorders used in modern transport.

Babbage is also credited with the **pilot**, or cow-catcher: a frame on the front of a locomotive designed to clear obstacles from the line (see [Charles Babbage: The Man](charles-babbage-the-man.md)).

### The ophthalmoscope

Around **1847** Babbage built a small instrument that used an angled mirror to direct light into the eye, allowing a physician to see the **retina** for the first time in a living patient. He passed it to the eye surgeon Thomas Wharton Jones, who did not pursue it.

{% hint style="warning" %}
Because Babbage's device went unpublished, the credit went to **Hermann von Helmholtz**, who independently invented the ophthalmoscope in 1851 and is usually named as its inventor today.
{% endhint %}

### Occulting lights for lighthouses

At sea, one steady light on the horizon looks much like another. In **1851** Babbage proposed **occulting lights**: lamps that are periodically covered and uncovered so that each lighthouse flashes its own numerical pattern, letting sailors identify it at night.

He sent the scheme to lighthouse authorities in several countries, and the principle of giving each light a distinctive signal became standard practice in coastal navigation.

### Codes and ciphers

Babbage was a keen cryptanalyst. In the 1850s he broke the **Vigenère autokey cipher**, a system then widely regarded as unbreakable, by analysing the repeated patterns it left in the ciphertext.

He never published his method — possibly because it was useful to British intelligence during the Crimean War — and the general technique was later made public by the Prussian officer **Friedrich Kasiski** in 1863.

### Mechanical notation

Designing engines with thousands of interacting parts forced Babbage to invent a way of describing them on paper. His **mechanical notation**, published in **1826**, used symbols to show how each part moved and how motion passed from one part to the next.

Babbage considered it one of his most important contributions, and it was essential to the drawings for both the Difference and Analytical Engines (see [The Analytical Engine](the-analytical-engine.md)).

### The cost of a letter

While researching _On the Economy of Machinery and Manufactures_, Babbage observed that the cost of moving a letter was tiny compared with the cost of handling, sorting, and charging for it by distance. This line of reasoning supported **Rowland Hill**'s case for a single low postage rate, which led to the **Penny Post** in 1840.

{% hint style="info" %}
Not all of Babbage's ideas were practical. He also proposed a machine that could play noughts and crosses (tic-tac-toe), and even considered touring it to raise money for the Analytical Engine — before concluding it would not earn enough.
{% endhint %}
