# Tommy Flowers and the Design

## Tommy Flowers and the Design

### The bottleneck

An earlier machine, the **Heath Robinson**, tried to find Lorenz wheel settings by comparing two paper tapes read at high speed. It worked in principle but was slow and unreliable: keeping two paper tapes synchronised at speed was mechanically hopeless, and the tapes often tore.

### An engineer's radical idea

**Tommy Flowers** (1905–1998), an engineer at the Post Office Research Station at Dollis Hill, proposed a radical solution: replace the second paper tape with an **electronic** store, generating the cipher-wheel patterns internally using **vacuum tubes** (thermionic valves).

|                | Heath Robinson                                   | Colossus                             |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------ |
| Message input  | Paper tape                                       | Paper tape                           |
| Wheel patterns | A second paper tape, kept in step with the first | Generated electronically by valves   |
| Main weakness  | Tapes drifted out of sync and tore               | None of the synchronisation problems |

At the time, many believed valves were too unreliable to use in such large numbers — a machine with over a thousand of them would surely fail constantly. Flowers, drawing on his telephone-exchange experience, knew the key insight:

{% hint style="info" %}
Valves mostly failed when switched on and off, due to thermal stress. If the machine was **left running continuously**, the valves were remarkably reliable. Flowers designed Colossus to stay powered on — and it worked.
{% endhint %}

### Built against the odds

Bletchley Park's management was sceptical and gave Flowers little official support, so he funded much of the early work himself. His team at Dollis Hill built the first **Colossus Mark 1** in about **ten months**, delivering it to Bletchley Park in December 1943; it was operational in February 1944.

An improved **Mark 2**, five times faster and using around 2,400 valves, followed in June 1944, just in time to support the Normandy invasion. Ten Colossi were in operation by the end of the war.
